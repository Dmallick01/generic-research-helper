TITLE: Simplex 3 — A Logarithmic Model of Pain Perception: Why Resolution Failure Amplifies Nociception Non-Linearly

ABSTRACT:
Standard pain models treat nociceptive signaling as approximately linear at clinical intensities. This paper proposes that SPM deficiency creates a logarithmic amplification effect on pain perception through three mechanisms: (1) reduced inhibitory interneuron activity in the dorsal horn, (2) microglial sensitization of second-order neurons, and (3) impaired descending inhibition via the serotonin-norepinephrine pathways. The logarithmic model predicts that small reductions in SPM levels produce disproportionately large increases in perceived pain intensity — with implications for why chronic pain is so resistant to conventional dose escalation.

INTRODUCTION:
The Weber-Fechner law describes the logarithmic relationship between stimulus intensity and perceived sensation. We apply this framework to pain in SPM-deficient states, proposing that baseline SPM levels set the "gain" of the nociceptive system. When SPMs are depleted, the system gain increases logarithmically — meaning the same inflammatory stimulus produces exponentially greater perceived pain.

THE LOGARITHMIC AMPLIFICATION MECHANISM:
Under normal SPM conditions: nociceptive input → first-order neuron → inhibitory interneuron modulation (GABA, glycine) → second-order neuron → thalamus → cortex. SPMs actively maintain inhibitory interneuron tone via resolvin D1 action on dorsal horn GABAergic circuits. When RvD1 levels fall: inhibitory interneuron suppression → disinhibition of second-order neurons → central sensitization. The key non-linearity: disinhibition does not scale proportionally with SPM depletion. Below a threshold SPM concentration, central sensitization becomes self-sustaining through NMDA receptor wind-up.

MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK:
Let P(t) = perceived pain intensity at time t. Let S(t) = SPM concentration. Let I(t) = nociceptive input. Proposed model: P(t) = I(t) × k / log(S(t) + 1), where k is a patient-specific gain constant. This predicts: as S(t) → 0, P(t) → infinity (unbounded pain amplification). As S(t) → normal physiological levels, P(t) ≈ I(t) × k (near-linear relationship restored). Clinical implication: doubling analgesic dose cannot compensate for SPM deficiency because the problem is gain amplification, not insufficient signal suppression.

CONNECTION TO SIMPLEX 2:
The POD model from Simplex 2 predicts perioperative SPM depletion. Simplex 3's model predicts that this same depletion amplifies pain non-linearly, explaining why POD patients often have heightened pain sensitivity — a phenomenon currently without mechanistic explanation in the literature.

DESCENDING INHIBITION PATHWAY:
The periaqueductal gray (PAG) → rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) → dorsal horn descending inhibition pathway is modulated by endocannabinoids and SPMs. Acetaminophen's AM404 metabolite (from Simplex 2) acts at this level. This creates a mechanistic bridge: acetaminophen partially compensates for SPM-deficient descending inhibition failure, explaining its analgesic efficacy beyond its anti-inflammatory action.

GAPS:
1. The mathematical model lacks empirical parameterization — k values by patient population are unknown.
2. SPM levels in dorsal horn tissue are technically difficult to measure in living patients.
3. The threshold SPM concentration for central sensitization transition is not established.
4. Whether the logarithmic relationship holds across pain modalities (neuropathic, visceral, somatic) is untested.

CONCLUSION:
The logarithmic gain model provides a unifying explanation for central sensitization, opioid tolerance, and treatment-resistant chronic pain. Simplex 4 will examine whether SPM supplementation (omega-3 loading, aspirin co-treatment) can reset the gain constant and restore near-linear pain processing.
